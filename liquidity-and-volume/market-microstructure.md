## The Anatomy of the market: The Order Book

Instead of old school buyers and sellers being face to face, the order book is an electronic auction house basically. Split up into 2 sections, the buyers and sellers of course. 
* The buyers ( bidders ) who are listed in descending order which shows the highest bidder or buyer who is willing to pay that price for the asset. Then as you go further down there are continuously lower and lower prices.

* The sellers ( askers/ offers ) are listed in ascending order which shows the lowest price someone is willing to part ways with their asset which is also closet to the buyers.
I would recommend looking this up but the big picture basically looks like buyers on the bottom, then sellers on top and if they agree on a price those orders get filled.

## The Bid-Ask Spread

It is simply the gap between the highest buyer and lowest seller
or simply: $$\text{Spread} = \text{Ask Price} - \text{Bid Price}$$

$$\text{Mid-Price} = \frac{\text{Best Bid} + \text{Best Ask}}{2}$$
This is useful to quants to find a quick fair value at any given moment, since if you are doing HFT then long calculations will slow you down even if you are more correct.( just trade offs, this isn't the only option to get fair prices fast ).

## Why?
Normal traders and the big institutions dont leave these gaps from the goodness of their heart they do it with risks involved as well of course. They are basically leaving their money or shares exposed
The width of a spread is dictated by three major factors:
* **Inventory Risk:** if a buyer agrees to buy at £100, they are at risk of being over bidden in the near future by someone with £100.05 for example, and they will lose money. Therefore if the gap or spread of the bid-ask is wide so their potential profit cushion covers the risk of holding the asset. (game theory involved)
* **Playing against older brother:** if you want to buy a stock at £50 and it instantly gets filled, most people would be happy since they got what they wanted, however getting what you wanted in trading isn't getting the best. You should feel nervous if such a thing happens since the person buying it probably knows something you down. They have faster and better computers which can get the latest news, best data and have the best mathematicians. Therefore if you widen the gap this is less likely to happen unless price changes very suddenly.
* **Volume:** if there is high volume and competition for these assets more people are trading and the gap shrinks to make sure its a HVN and liquidity is assured. Vise versa for lower popular / lower volume / lower competition stocks the gaps are wider to compensate for the fact that there is low liquidity.
