## Post-Earnings Announcement Drift (PEAD)

First documented by Ray Ball and Philip Brown in 1968, PEAD is one of the most famous and persistent anomalies in corporate finance. 


## Definition and why

In a theoretical efficient market, when a company releases its quarterly earnings / financial reports that are way off to what analysts predict then in theory people will see the asset is worth more / less than what they expected hence its price "should" in theory jump very fast to the new fair price and stop for a while.

However prices tend to underreact to earning surprises in the short term instead making the asset "drift " in that direction slowly rather than very fast(typically before next announcement). However this drift can persist for weeks or even months

**Positive Surprise:** The stock gap-ups on earnings day, but then continues to steadily drift upward for 60–90 days.

**Negative Surprise:** The stock gap-downs, but then continues to bleed downward over the next quarter.

## How to take advantage of such an indicator:


Instead of trying to predict earnings before they happen, a PEAD strategy enters after the announcement:

* **Quantify the surprise:** When earnings drop, measure the Standardized Unexpected Earnings (SUE) or how many standard deviations actual earnings are below or above what analysts predicted.
* **Filter Extreme Deciles:** Rank all reporting stocks. Focus only on the top 10% (massive positive surprise) and bottom 10% (negative).
* **Ride the drift:** Simple strategy, go long on the top tier positive surprise stocks. And vise versa go short( or avoid ) for bottom tier surprises. 
* **Exit on Schedule:** Hold the positions for a fixed time (usually 30-60 trading days) and exit before the next earnings cycle begins to avoid event risk.

## Why does this work?

We are humans at the end of the day and this anomaly persists simply because of that fact. 
* **Anchoring:** the big investors and or analysts tend to anchor to their prior beliefs about a company so when new data arrives, they update their financial models a bit at first as well. They tend to raise their earnings estimates over the following 2–3 months. Each time an analyst upgrades their price target, it injects a fresh wave of buying into the stock, feeding the drift.
* **Execution Drag:** The big players cannot simply dump all their capital into buying or selling because of liquidity so they will also slowly buy or sell within a time frame as well to not be victims to slippage so if you take this into account you can make some money( assuming you know what they do )


## Noted Limitations:
* **Too Fast for you:** Thanks to algorithms today which can take into account PEAD very fast the window of opportunity is much smaller, as we know aa drift can happen up to 90 days, but that drift could be completely bought up by HF Traders within days.
* **Tail Risk:** This just comes down to macro vs micro, you invest in a stock because you see the earnings but if federal reserve suddenly shocks the market with an interest rate hike, or a geopolitical event triggers a market-wide sell-off, broad market panic will drag down Stock A regardless of its stellar earnings. Market-wide macro momentum can completely mask or temporarily destroy the individual stock's drift.
* **Up against the best:** if you are planning to make money of big stocks like Amazon or Microsoft using PEAD, well you are probably going up against 40 analysts from Wall street who are constantly monitoring these quarterly earnings pages for updates, so prices will change very fast since a lot of people have their eyes on them. 
* **Small Cap:** While small caps do have fewer eyes, retail traders still face a problem Liquidity & Transaction Costs. Small-cap stocks have wider bid-ask spreads and lower trading volume. While the drift is larger in small caps, trading costs can eat up a chunk of a retail trader's profit margin. It shines best for small/mid-cap quant funds, but retail traders must be mindful of execution costs when trading smaller names.
